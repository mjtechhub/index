QoS Explained

QoS (Quality of Service) refers to mechanisms that manage the treatment of different traffic classes, especially when network resources (bandwidth) are constrained.

Critical Rule: QoS does not create bandwidth. QoS manages how available bandwidth and hardware queues are utilized when congestion occurs.

QoS Components

Implementing QoS involves several distinct processes:

  • Classification: Identifying and categorizing traffic (e.g., matching voice traffic based on ports or IPs).
  • Marking: Altering the packet header (such as the IP DSCP field) so downstream routers recognize its priority without needing to deeply inspect it again.
  • Queuing and Scheduling: Placing traffic into different hardware buffers (queues) and deciding which queue gets transmitted next. High-priority queues are serviced first to minimize latency.

Shaping vs. Policing

When enforcing bandwidth limits, routers use two different conceptual approaches:

  • Traffic Shaping: Buffers and delays excess traffic to smooth out bursts, forcing traffic to conform to a specific rate. This adds latency but minimizes drops.
  • Policing: Typically drops (or remarks to a lower priority) traffic that exceeds configured limits. It does not buffer.

Practical Categories

A typical enterprise QoS policy categorizes traffic to ensure latency-sensitive applications aren't starved by large downloads:

  • Voice/Video: High priority, strictly low-latency queues (e.g., VoIP).
  • Business Applications: Guaranteed minimum bandwidth.
  • Bulk Downloads / Backups: Best-effort or scavenger class, utilizing whatever bandwidth is left over.
