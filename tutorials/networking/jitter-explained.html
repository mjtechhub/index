Jitter Explained

Jitter is the variation in packet delay over time. While latency measures the time it takes for a packet to reach its destination, jitter measures how consistent that time is.

Understanding Jitter

Consider four packets sent at a steady rate:

  • Packet 1: 20 ms latency
  • Packet 2: 21 ms latency
  • Packet 3: 65 ms latency
  • Packet 4: 19 ms latency

Packet 3 experienced a significant delay compared to the others. This variation is jitter. High jitter destroys real-time applications like VoIP (Voice over IP), video calls, and gaming because the data arrives out of rhythm, causing choppy audio or video artifacts.

Jitter Buffers

To combat this, applications use a jitter buffer. This conceptually holds incoming packets slightly longer than necessary before playing them to the user, smoothing out the inconsistent arrival times. However, if the buffer is too large, it adds unacceptable overall delay to the conversation.

Acceptable Thresholds

There is no universal threshold like "30 ms jitter is always bad." Acceptable values depend entirely on the application, the codec being used, buffering capabilities, and network design. Standard ping tests alone do not provide a complete or reliable jitter measurement; specialized monitoring tools and active probes (like IP SLA) are required to measure it accurately.
